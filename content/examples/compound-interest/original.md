Compound interest is interest calculated on both the money you originally saved and the interest it has already earned. That single twist, earnings that start earning themselves, is what turns slow savings into surprisingly fast growth over long periods.

With simple interest, you earn the same amount every year: a fixed rate applied only to your starting deposit. Put $1,000 aside at 5% simple interest and you gain exactly $50 each year, reaching $1,500 after ten years. With compound interest, each year's earnings are added to your balance, so the next year's rate applies to a larger amount. That same $1,000 at 5% compounded yearly grows to about $1,629 after ten years. The gap looks small at first, but it widens every year because growth builds on growth.

Three ingredients control the outcome. The principal is the money you start with. The rate is the yearly percentage your savings earn. Time is the number of years the money stays invested, and it matters most of all, because compounding is an exponential process: each extra year multiplies everything that came before. Adding money regularly, called contributions, accelerates things further, since every deposit starts its own compounding journey.

Consider saving $200 each month at 6% yearly for 30 years. You will have put in $72,000 of your own money, but the balance reaches roughly $201,000, so about $129,000 of it is growth doing the heavy lifting. Start ten years later with the same monthly amount and rate, and you end up with far less, even though you missed only a third of the deposits. That is the real lesson: with compounding, early years are worth more than later ones, because early money has the most time to multiply.
