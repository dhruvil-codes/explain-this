A vaccine is a safe practice run for your immune system. Your body studies a harmless stand-in. Later it beats the real germ fast.

It works in steps:

- **Meet the stand-in.** The shot holds a weakened germ, a piece of one, or building plans for one harmless piece. It cannot cause the full illness. It carries markers that identify the germ.
- **Raise the alarm.** Guard cells near the shot grab the markers and call for help. Your arm may feel sore. You may feel tired or feverish for a bit. That is your defenses mobilizing.
- **Start training.** Messenger cells carry the markers to small glands called lymph nodes. There, helper cells confirm the threat and approve a full response.
- **Build blockers.** Some cells turn into factories. They release antibodies, Y-shaped proteins that latch onto the germ's markers and tag it for removal. This takes a week or two.
- **Clear and remember.** The stand-in is cleared. Most fighters stand down. But memory cells stay behind, often for years. They remember the exact markers.
- **Win the rematch.** If the real germ arrives, memory cells skip the slow learning. They multiply within hours and flood the body with blockers. You often never feel sick.

For example, a flu shot teaches your body the flu's markers in autumn. When winter flu arrives, your defenses already know it and stop it early. A later booster dose refreshes the memory.
