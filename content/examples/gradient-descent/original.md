Gradient descent is the workhorse learning method behind most modern machine learning. It answers a simple question: how should a model's settings be adjusted so that its mistakes get smaller? Imagine the model's total error as a hilly landscape, where every possible combination of settings is a location and the height is the error there. Learning means walking downhill until you reach a valley.

Training starts with a random guess for the settings, called the weights. The model makes predictions on some examples, and a cost function boils all the mistakes down to a single number: the error score. The higher the number, the worse the model is doing. The key mathematical tool is the gradient, which measures the slope of the error landscape at the current location. It tells you, for each setting, which direction would raise the error and how steeply.

The update rule then steps the opposite way: each weight moves a little bit downhill, against the slope. The size of that step is controlled by the learning rate. If the learning rate is too small, training crawls and can take impractically long; if it is too large, the steps overshoot the valley and the error can bounce around or even grow. Choosing it well is one of the most important practical decisions in training, and many popular methods adjust it automatically as they go.

This measure-and-step loop repeats for many iterations over the data. Each pass nudges the weights a little further downhill, and the error usually falls in a curve that drops fast at first and then flattens out. For simple bowl-shaped problems there is a single lowest point and the method is guaranteed to approach it with a sensible step size. For deep networks the landscape is full of ridges and shallow dips, so the walk may settle into a local minimum, a dip that is not the deepest possible. In practice these dips still give models that work remarkably well, which is why this humble downhill walk powers so much of today's technology.
