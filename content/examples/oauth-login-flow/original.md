When a website offers "Log in with Google" or "Continue with Apple", it uses a standard called OAuth 2.0 so that you can prove who you are without handing your password to every site you visit. Your password stays with the big provider you already trust, and the smaller site receives only a limited pass that says what it may do on your behalf.

It starts when you click the login button on an app, say a photo-printing site. The site redirects your browser to the provider with a note describing itself and exactly which permissions, called scopes, it is asking for, such as viewing your email address. You then interact only with the provider: you log in there if needed and see a permission screen that names the app and lists what it wants. Nothing you type goes to the printing site.

If you approve, the provider sends your browser back to the app carrying a short-lived authorization code. This code is not the final pass; it is more like a claim ticket. The app then contacts the provider directly, server to server, and swaps the code for tokens, proving its identity with a secret that only it and the provider know. This back-channel swap is the heart of the flow's security, because the secret never travels through the browser where it could be seen.

The provider answers with an access token, a string the app attaches to its requests when fetching your data, and usually a refresh token for getting new access tokens later without bothering you. Access tokens expire, typically within minutes or hours, which limits the damage if one leaks. The app uses the token to ask for just the agreed data, for example your name and email, and creates your account or logs you in. It never learns your password, and you can revoke its access at any time from the provider's settings page, which instantly turns its tokens useless.
